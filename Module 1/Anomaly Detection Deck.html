Part 1 — Foundations

What counts as an anomaly

What is anomaly detection?

Identifying data points, events, or observations that deviate significantly from a dataset's normal behavior.

Three types — memorize these

  • point  global outliers
  • contextual  conditional outliers
  • collective  groups of outliers
point anomaly

Anomalous on its own

A single instance that deviates from the rest — no context required to call it odd.

Example: a credit-card charge far outside normal spend.

contextual anomaly

Normal value, wrong context

The point looks fine alone — it's anomalous only given its context (time, season, location).

Example: heavy traffic — routine at rush hour, suspicious at 3 a.m.

collective anomaly

The pattern is the anomaly

Individual points look normal; the collection of them is anomalous together.

Example: a DoS attack, or a slow drift in sensor readings.

Part 2 — Evolution of methods

Assumptions traded for flexibility

ERA 1 · 1980s–90s

Statistical & probabilistic

  • Assumption: data follows a distribution (e.g. Gaussian)
  • Z-score / 3-sigma rule — flag when |z|=|x−μ|σ>3
  • Boxplots (interquartile range)

Pro

Simple, interpretable.

Con

Fails when data isn't that distribution, or is high-dimensional.

ERA 2 · 2000s

Classical machine learning

  • Proximity: k-NN (far from neighbors) · LOF (local density vs. neighbors)
  • Boundary: One-Class SVM learns a boundary around "normal"
  • Ensemble: Isolation Forest — random splits isolate anomalies faster

Traded: no fixed distribution — but normal data must cluster / be dense. LOF and Isolation Forest are your lab.

ERA 3 · 2015–present

Deep learning & modern approaches

  • Why: unstructured data (images, audio, logs) + the curse of dimensionality
  • Reconstruction: autoencoders rebuild "normal" — the error is the anomaly score s(x)=‖x−x^‖2
  • Generative: GANs — anomalies look "fake" to the discriminator

Traded: normality becomes a learnable manifold.

HOW TO CHOOSE · you'll reuse this for the project

A three-question chooser

1 · Labels?

Almost never. Treat it as unsupervised; keep any labels only to evaluate.

2 · Structure?

Tabular → Isolation Forest / LOF.
Sequential → autoencoder / sequence model.

3 · Interpretable?

Need to explain it? Start classical. Need raw power on complex data? Go deep.

Module 1 — measuring detectors

But how do we know it works?

THE VOCABULARY · four numbers that actually matter

Precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC

TP caught anomaly  ·  FP false alarm  ·  FN missed anomaly

Precision

P=TPTP+FP

Of everything you flagged, how much was truly anomalous? — "can I trust my alarms?"

Recall

R=TPTP+FN

Of all true anomalies, how many did you catch? — "am I missing real problems?"

F1-score

F1=2·P·RP+R

The harmonic mean of the two — punishes ignoring either.

PR-AUC

AUCPR=∫01P(r)dr

Precision over recall across every threshold, as one area — the honest summary under heavy imbalance.

Part 3 — Our research

Temporal attention autoencoders for IoT

Signal vs. noise in Industrial IoT

Sensors emit massive, high-dimensional, noisy streams. Models over-react to benign operational noise → frequent false alarms.

Objective: a robust unsupervised model that filters background noise to catch genuine, early-warning signs of machine failure.

SENSOR STREAM · raise the noise, then turn attention on

Why attention cuts false alarms

false alarms — real fault —

A hybrid LSTM-autoencoder with attention

Autoencoder

Compress & reconstruct normal operation.

LSTM

Handles the sequence — catches contextual drift over time.

Attention

The innovation: focus on critical time-steps, ignore noisy segments.

Results on SWaT

  • Benchmarked on Secure Water Treatment (SWaT) — industrial standard
  • Attention reduced false alarms ~18% vs. a standard autoencoder
  • Especially strong on collective anomalies — slow drifts = machine wear
